## 00:00 The game gets a shape
- Title screen, a theatrical-cut intro for every scene (you watch the studio's version fail before you touch it),
  save data, a scene-select board, pause menu, volume, fullscreen, credits.
- Hints: the solver finds a cheapest winning cut, diffs it against the player's strips and names one edit
  ("Cut SODA AD from ACE's strip"), with the clip pulsing.
- Stars are a bitmask per scene: printed, at or under par, and a studio note such as *keep the money shot*. Some
  bonuses need a different solution from the par one, so a scene can be worth solving twice.

![The studio's cut fails first](img/02-theatrical-cut-fails.png)

## 00:10 Levels by proof
The level format is text. `unreal.MJDevLibrary.solve_levels(text)` returns, for each scene, whether the theatrical cut
fails, how many distinct winning cuts exist at each edit count, and sample solutions. A scene goes in when the
theatrical cut fails for a readable reason, the minimum equals par, and the bonus is reachable. A few things the solver
caught that I had wrong on paper:
- "The Scenic Route" had a second solution where the hero bumps a wall instead of falling in the pit. Fixed by widening
  the pit.
- "Two Man Job" had no solution at all: I had put the laser on the wrong channel in the ASCII map.
- "Look Both Ways" was already won by the theatrical cut, because I started the goon one tile too far west and the car
  ran him over unprompted.
- The finale exploded past three million states at four edits. Three edits: 396,020 states, 43 distinct solutions,
  2.2 seconds.

Reel 1 teaches cut, reel 2 splice, reel 3 restore. My favourite scene is "The Monologue": the Baron's clips are
contractually locked, but the studio deleted one, and restoring it keeps him talking until the hero is out of the room.

## 05:00 It stops looking like boxes
![Reel 3 with the kit, props and a hint](img/05-reel3-showdown-hint.png)

- The set is now built from a 20-piece bevelled kit (Blender, scripted, by the Codex sub-agent). Walls that border
  the floor are blocks; the far edge of every room gets a painted "flat" with timber bracing, like a real film set.
- Figurines and desk props are Meshy models made from concept images. The scissors and coffee mug are deliberately
  huge: they are what tells you the set is a toy.
- A 900 mm virtual sensor gives a tilt-shift depth of field. The cutting room view uses a narrower aperture than the
  title, because readability beats prettiness while you are editing.
- Lasers and gates wear the colour of whatever controls them.

![The kit, as rendered by the sub-agent that made it](img/06-blender-kit-sheet.png)

## 08:13 The PC crashed
Shane restored Windows from a restore point and asked whether anything was lost. What I checked, in order:
`git fsck` (clean), every one of the 289 large files against the local LFS store (all present), the working tree against
HEAD. System Restore had rolled back exactly the file types it monitors: the built DLL (to yesterday 19:02), one `.ini`,
and it deleted `MJLevelData.inl`, the file that holds every level, because `.inl` is on its list and the file did not
exist at the restore point. The level data was in the last commit, and the uncommitted Reel 3 text was still in my
scratch folder. Two commits had never reached GitHub because the push was mid-flight when the machine died.
Recovery took four minutes: restore two files from git, re-append Reel 3, push, rebuild. The session's own connection
to the editor did not come back, so I wrote a 40-line shell client for Unreal MCP and carried on through that.
